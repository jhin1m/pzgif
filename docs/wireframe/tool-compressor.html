How GIF compression actually works

A GIF is not a video. It is a stack of still images, each one limited to a palette of at most 256 colors, played back on a timer. That format was designed in 1987, and it is the reason a five-second clip that would be 300 KB as an MP4 can arrive as a 6 MB GIF. There are only four levers that make a GIF smaller: fewer pixels per frame, fewer frames, fewer colors, and better encoding of the parts that do not change between frames. Every honest GIF compressor is pulling some combination of those four.

Most online compressors pull the crude ones. They drop the palette to 64 colors, apply a heavy-handed dither, and hand you a file that is technically smaller and visibly worse — banded skies, speckled gradients, smeared edges. PZGIF uses gifski, which builds the palette per frame instead of once for the whole animation and applies temporal dithering across frames rather than within a single one. In practice that means you can hold quality at a setting where other tools have already started to fall apart.

Which setting should you change first?

Width. Halving the width of a GIF removes three quarters of the pixels and usually cuts the file by roughly the same proportion, with no visible damage as long as the GIF is not already small. Try that before you touch the palette. After width, lower Lossy to somewhere between 20 and 40 — it lets the encoder treat nearly-identical pixels as identical across frames, which is where most of the remaining savings hide. Reduce Colors last, and only for flat artwork, screenshots and line drawings. Photographic content below 128 colors starts to band immediately.

When a GIF is the wrong format

If the destination accepts MP4 or WebP, use them. The same animation as an MP4 is typically five to ten times smaller than a well-compressed GIF, because a real video codec can describe motion instead of re-listing pixels. We show both alternatives on the result panel for exactly this reason. Use GIF when the platform demands it — Discord emoji, Slack, email signatures, some forums and wikis — and use a video format everywhere else.
